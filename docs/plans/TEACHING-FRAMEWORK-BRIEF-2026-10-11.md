# Brief: a framework and a story for the investing game (R15, R16), 2026-10-11

For Stephen's call before any build. Written by the lead after his three plays of rc3 and rc4 and a check of the game against the standards that exist for this age.

## 1. The verdict

The engine is a sound toy market: seeded towns, six shops and a basket, moods, cards, a ledger that reconciles, fixtures that replay. Nothing in it is wrong.

The experience never asks the player to make a move. Every chapter hands the player more coins than the goal needs, so the goal can be met from the Jar alone and investing, spreading out, selling and pulling money back are all optional. Optional moves on a quiet screen are what "nothing to it" feels like. Chapter 1 starts with 100 coins and a goal of 30. Chapter 2 starts with 200 and a goal of 60. A child who never buys a share wins both.

The standards say the same thing from the other side. The 2021 National Standards for Personal Financial Education put it in one line for 4th grade: investing is for long-term goals that saving alone will not reach, and saving is for short-term goals and emergencies. A game that teaches investing has to put the player in front of a goal that saving alone will not reach, then teach the moves that get there.

So the fix is not more copy on the same screens. It is three things: a backbone of moves taken from the standards, a stake in every chapter that makes those moves necessary, and a story form that makes a child want to see what happens next.

## 2. The framework, four parts

**What to teach: the 2021 National Standards for Personal Financial Education** (Council for Economic Education with the Jump$tart Coalition). Six areas with benchmarks at 4th, 8th and 12th grade. The 4th grade Investing outcomes, quoted: "4-1a. Explain why people invest their money. 4-1b. Identify long-term financial goals that are most likely to be achieved by people who regularly invest their money over many years. 4-2a. Identify the similarities and differences between saving and investing. 4-2b. Provide examples of financial goals that are suited for saving versus investing." The 4th grade Saving outcomes: saving is a choice between spending now and later, people differ in how they feel about saving, savers earn interest. For the 11 year olds the 8th grade Investing outcomes are the ceiling: a stock makes you a part owner; pooled funds hold many shares at once; different investments carry different risk; spreading out reduces risk; returns compound over years. The game already contains every one of these ideas. It never names them as things the player does.

**How this age learns: the CFPB building blocks.** The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's framework says ages 6 to 12 are when financial habits and norms form, mostly by watching how family and peers handle money. That is the design argument for the four neighbors: Ada spreads out, Bo keeps a jar, Dev picks one shop, Cass follows the news. Today they are silent coin bars. In the story they become the way the child learns each move, by watching a neighbor make it and then being asked to try.

**The loop: the Stock Market Game** (SIFMA Foundation, grades 4 to 12, about 600,000 students a year). Teams run a pretend portfolio toward a long-term goal through research, decide, watch, review, rebalance. A 2008 randomized study by Learning Point Associates found players scored higher on financial literacy and mathematics tests than non-players. The part to borrow is the review and rebalance step at every stop, with a reason to rebalance.

**The form: The Great Piggy Bank Adventure** (T. Rowe Price with Disney, 2009, ages 8 to 14). A story game: pick a dream goal, save toward it, a wolf raises prices, learn to spread out, reach the dream. Goal, villain, moves, payoff. That is the shape Stephen described when he said write and animate a story.

## 3. The moves (the curriculum as verbs)

Every chapter teaches one or two moves. A move is a story beat, a prompt with a one tap way to do it, a recap line that names what it did, and a scrapbook stamp. Nothing is optional any more: the chapter's goal cannot be reached without the move.

| Move | What the child does | Standard | Chapter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Set the goal | Pick the thing, the amount, the date | Saving 4 | every chapter opens with it |
| Keep soon money safe | Put the goal's near money in the Jar | Saving 4, Investing 4-2 | 1 |
| Own a piece | Buy shares of one shop and watch the price | Investing 4-1, 8 (part owner) | 1 |
| Spread out | Hold the Basket or several shops so one problem matters less | Investing 8 (pooled, risk) | 2 |
| Check in and rebalance | At every stop: review, then keep, add, or move | Stock Market Game loop | 2 onward |
| Pull money toward the goal | As the date nears, sell on a schedule and move coins to the Jar | Investing 4-2 (which money goes where) | 2 and 3, then always |
| Spend on the goal | Goal day: pay for it from the Jar | Saving 4 | every chapter ends with it |
| Keep a buffer | Money for surprises, separate from the goal | Saving 4 (emergencies) | 3 |
| Reinvest payments | Use a dividend to buy more shares | Investing 8 (compounding) | 4 |
| Hold or sell in a slump | Decide with the goal date in view, no judgment | Investing 8 (risk) | 5 |
| Mind costs and rising prices | Read the cost card, see what coins can buy | Investing 8, Spending 4 | 6 |

The move Stephen named as missing, pull money toward the goal, has no screen today. The engine supports it (sell, Jar in) and the scoring rewards it (the goal fit stamp), but no screen ever says "your trip is a year away and your money is still in shares; move some now". That becomes a scheduled decision card at the goal fit checkpoints, with three real buttons: move some to the Jar now, keep growing, decide next stop. The recap then shows what the choice did.

## 4. The story

Each chapter is an episode with the same spine:

1. **Title card.** The town, the chapter name, the goal and the date.
2. **Cold open.** The narrator sets the stake in two lines. "Your bicycle costs 80. You have 60. Saving alone gets you 60."
3. **The town's year.** The show as it is now, with the narrator's one line per month and the neighbors visibly doing their thing.
4. **The turn.** A decision card the narrator poses, always a move from the table: spread out, pull toward the goal, hold or sell, reinvest.
5. **The payoff.** Goal day, funded or short, and what the neighbors did.
6. **The closing card.** The move you made, in one sentence, stamped into the scrapbook.

The narrator is a new character because the keepers must never talk about money (their spec, and a good rule). A town clerk, a librarian, a grandparent on the bench: someone who keeps the town's notice board and can read the Gazette aloud. Three or four poses and six title cards are the art. The words are the lead's, or Astra's if Stephen wants her voice. Animation is the show, which already plays months as frames; the narrator's lines ride on those frames, and the decision cards become scenes.

## 5. The stake (Stephen's number to rule on)

A goal reachable from the Jar alone makes every move optional. The principle for the retune: in chapters 2 to 6 the goal sits above what saving alone can reach by the date, so the child has to grow the money, spread it, and pull it back in time. For example, chapter 2 starts with 200 coins and a 60 coin goal; make the goal 230 in 36 months with no contributions, and the Basket's typical year does the rest. The exact numbers are content constants (DECK bump, fixtures rewritten in the same commit) and need Stephen's yes. Chapter 1 stays a practice year with the stake carried by the story rather than the number.

## 6. Phases

- **A. The moves layer** (about one build day, no content numbers): the review card at every stop with the three moves as buttons, the pull toward the goal card at the goal fit checkpoints, narrator copy for chapters 2 to 6, episode title and closing cards in text, stamps renamed as moves. Engine and fixtures untouched. Playable the same evening.
- **B. The story and the art** (as Stephen's art lands): narrator sprites, title cards, lines on the show frames, the decision cards as scenes, music by feel.
- **C. The stake and the playtest** (half a day plus the children): goals retuned under the principle above, DECK bump, fixtures rewritten, then five to eight children per the playtest plan.

Each phase is a plan the lead writes, one Opus builder, and Stephen's play before the next.

## 7. What Stephen decides

1. The framework: the 2021 National Standards as the backbone, the CFPB lens for the neighbors, the Stock Market Game loop, the Piggy Bank story form. Yes, or a different backbone.
2. The narrator: who they are. A name and an animal, or Astra proposes three.
3. The stake principle: goals above what saving alone reaches. Yes, or keep the toy numbers.
4. The order: A then B then C, or B first because the art is arriving.
